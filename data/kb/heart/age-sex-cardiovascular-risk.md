Age is a non-modifiable risk factor for coronary artery disease. Risk rises
sharply after about age 45 in men and about age 55 in women, reflecting the
cumulative effect of lifelong exposure to blood pressure, cholesterol and
metabolic load on the arterial wall.

Sex modifies how risk presents. Men tend to develop obstructive coronary
disease earlier on average, while women's risk accelerates after menopause.
Atypical symptom presentation is also more common in women, which is one
reason symptom-only assessment is never sufficient on its own.

In screening models, age and sex therefore act mainly as background risk
adjusters: they shift the prior probability that other findings (chest pain
character, exercise response, ST changes) reflect significant coronary
stenosis. A higher-risk screening pattern in an older adult warrants the
same next step as in a younger adult — clinician review — but the urgency
of follow-up is generally greater.

These factors cannot be changed, so guidance focuses on what can: blood
pressure control, lipid management, smoking cessation, physical activity
and diabetes control, as summarised in the American Heart Association's
risk-factor materials.
