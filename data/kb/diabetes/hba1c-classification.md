Glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) reflects average glycemia over the preceding two
to three months and anchors modern classification: values above 6.5 percent
indicate diabetes, 5.7 to 6.4 percent indicate pre-diabetes, and lower
values are in the non-diabetic range — each confirmed by repeat testing
under ADA standards.

Because it integrates months of exposure into one number, HbA1c is
typically the single strongest contributor in diabetes screening patterns,
and the feature most likely to separate the three classes (non-diabetic,
pre-diabetic, diabetic) from one another.

Cohort-standardised HbA1c values, as used in this screening pipeline, express
how far an individual sits from the cohort mean rather than a laboratory
percentage — direction and magnitude of deviation carry the signal. A large
positive deviation aligned with diabetic-range classification is the
clearest single-feature prompt for confirmatory venous testing.

Any flag dominated by HbA1c should be followed by laboratory confirmation
and clinician review, since anemia, hemoglobin variants and assay differences
can all perturb the reading.
